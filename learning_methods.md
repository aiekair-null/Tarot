# Learning and research methods

A reference, not a script -- read only the section that fits what you're stuck on. Organized by category
so a single `grep` or a quick skim finds the right part without reading the whole file.

## Framing the question

- State the actual question before searching, not just the topic name -- "what am I trying to find out?"
- Define any vague or abstract term in your own words before you search for it.
- Break a big or fuzzy question into 2-4 smaller, checkable sub-questions.
- Separate empirical claims (a source can confirm or refute them) from conceptual ones (a matter of
  definition, values, or opinion -- no source "settles" these).
- Name what you already believe about the topic before researching it, so you notice if you're just
  confirming it rather than actually checking it.
- Write down what evidence would change your mind, before you start looking.

## Searching effectively

- Start broad, then narrow once you see what vocabulary the field actually uses.
- Use the specific jargon a field uses once you learn it -- it surfaces better results than plain language.
- Quote an exact phrase to find pages using that precise wording.
- Add `site:` to search one domain, or `-word` to exclude a term that keeps pulling up noise.
- Search a question from more than one angle (a proponent's framing and a critic's framing) rather than one
  phrasing you're comfortable with.
- Follow citation trails: a good source's own references often lead to better ones.
- Check a topic's Wikipedia "References" section as a map of primary sources, even if you don't quote
  Wikipedia itself.
- If a search returns nothing useful after a few tries, the terms are probably wrong, not the topic empty --
  try a different vocabulary before concluding there's nothing to find.
- Narrow with more specific terms when you're drowning in generic results; broaden when you're getting
  nothing useful at all.

## Evaluating sources

- Check who wrote it and what expertise or stake they have in the claim.
- Check when it was written or last updated -- a stale source on a fast-moving topic can be confidently
  wrong.
- Prefer a primary source (the original study, project, post, filing) over someone's summary of it, when you
  can reach it.
- Corroborate a specific claim across more than one independent source before treating it as solid.
- On forums or social platforms, weigh a claim by whether replies corroborate or dispute it, not by how
  confident it sounds.
- Distinguish a fact from an interpretation of a fact, even within the same sentence.
- Notice financial or ideological incentives a source has to present something a particular way.
- A claim that "everyone knows" is worth checking anyway -- popularity isn't evidence.

## Active reading and comprehension

- Skim first for structure (headings, abstract, conclusion) before reading closely.
- Ask a specific question of the text before you start reading it, so you're reading to answer something.
- After each section, state in one sentence what it argued, without looking back.
- Slow down specifically where you notice confusion rather than ploughing through it.
- Distinguish what the author claims from what they actually demonstrated.
- Read the conclusion and the introduction first for a long or technical piece, then decide how much of the
  middle you actually need.

## Note-taking systems

- Cornell notes: a narrow margin for cues/questions, a wide column for notes, a summary at the bottom.
- Atomic notes (Zettelkasten-style): one idea per note, written so it stands alone, linked to related notes.
- Outline method: nested bullets by level of generality -- fast to take, easy to scan later.
- Mapping method: branch sub-ideas visually off a central concept -- good for showing relationships.
- Charting method: a table with one column per source and one row per question, for comparing several
  sources on the same points side by side.
- Write notes in your own words, not copied phrasing -- restating forces you to actually understand it.
- Record the source with every note at the time you take it; retrofitting citations later wastes time and
  loses precision.

## Memory and encoding

- Retrieval practice (the testing effect): trying to recall something from memory strengthens it far more
  than re-reading it does.
- Spaced repetition: review at increasing intervals instead of all at once; spacing beats cramming for
  anything you need to retain.
- Elaborative interrogation: ask "why would this be true?" and answer it yourself.
- Self-explanation: explain each step to yourself as you go, not just at the end.
- Dual coding: pair verbal information with a diagram, image, or spatial layout.
- The generation effect: producing an answer yourself (even a guess) before checking it encodes it better
  than being told the answer outright.
- Chunking: group related items into a smaller number of meaningful units.
- The method of loci: attach items to a sequence of locations you know well, when order and recall of
  discrete items both matter.
- Interleaving: mix related topics or problem types in one session rather than blocking all of one kind
  together -- harder in the moment, better retained.
- Desirable difficulty: a technique that feels slower or harder (recall, interleaving) often produces better
  long-term learning than one that feels smooth (re-reading, highlighting).
- Teach it: explaining a topic to someone else (or writing it up as if to teach it) exposes gaps re-reading
  never would.

## Synthesis and organizing findings

- Build a matrix: sources as rows, claims or questions as columns, to see agreement and conflict at a
  glance.
- Write a one-paragraph synthesis after every few sources, not just at the very end -- it keeps you from
  losing the thread on a long research task.
- Explicitly note contradictions between sources instead of silently picking the one you read last.
- Separate "established," "contested," and "unknown/untested" when summarizing a body of findings.
- Concept mapping: draw the relationships between ideas, not just a list of them.
- Argument mapping: lay out a claim, its supporting reasons, and objections to each reason, as a structure.

## Metacognition and self-regulation

- Periodically self-quiz on what you've covered without looking at your notes, then check against them.
- Calibrate: before checking, estimate your own confidence in an answer, then see how accurate that estimate
  was.
- The Feynman technique: try to explain the idea simply; wherever the explanation breaks down marks exactly
  what you don't understand yet.
- Keep a running list of open questions and gaps, not just of things you've confirmed.
- Notice when you're spending time because it's comfortable (re-reading something familiar) rather than
  because it's useful (testing or extending your understanding).
- Review errors specifically, not just successes -- a wrong answer examined closely teaches more than a
  right one skimmed past.

## Handling abstract, unusual, or contested topics

- Define terms explicitly before debating or researching them -- much disagreement is really disagreement
  about definitions.
- Steelman each position before evaluating it: state the strongest version of a view, not the easiest one to
  knock down.
- Identify the underlying assumptions a position depends on, and check whether they're shared or disputed.
- Use a thought experiment or edge case to pressure-test a general claim.
- Draw analogies to a more familiar domain to get traction, but check explicitly where the analogy breaks
  down -- that's often where the real insight is.
- Distinguish "no one has studied this" from "this has been studied and the answer is unsettled" from "this
  isn't the kind of question evidence can settle."
- When sources disagree and neither looks obviously wrong, report the disagreement itself as a finding
  rather than forcing a conclusion.
- For a genuinely novel or niche idea, look for the closest adjacent, better-studied topic as a starting
  point rather than searching for the exact idea and finding nothing.

## Deliberate practice

- Practice at the edge of current ability -- comfortable repetition of what's already easy doesn't build new
  skill.
- Get feedback as close to immediately as possible, rather than batching it up.
- Break a complex skill into components and drill the weakest one specifically.
- Set a concrete target for a practice session, not just "get better at X."
- Rest and recovery between hard practice sessions is part of the method, not a break from it.

## Time and attention management

- Time-box a task (a fixed block, e.g. 25-50 minutes) rather than leaving it open-ended -- a deadline, even
  a self-imposed one, concentrates effort.
- Single-task: one keyword, one question, one source at a time, rather than skimming several in parallel.
- Take a short break between blocks; sustained unbroken focus degrades quality even when the clock keeps
  running.
- Decide the smallest next concrete action instead of restating the whole remaining task.

## Writing up findings

- Lead with the answer or the current best understanding, then the supporting detail -- not a chronological
  account of the search.
- State confidence explicitly: certain / likely / contested / unknown -- don't flatten everything to the
  same tone of certainty.
- Note what's missing or unresolved as plainly as what's established.
- Keep the specific claim and its source together -- a finding detached from where it came from loses most
  of its value.
- Before finishing, check the write-up actually answers the original question as it was first framed, not a
  nearby question that turned out easier to answer.
